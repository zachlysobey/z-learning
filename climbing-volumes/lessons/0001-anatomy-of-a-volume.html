Anatomy of a volume

Name the parts, then go measure the two triangles in your basement.

A plywood volume is a hollow shell: a few flat faces meeting at angled edges, with an open base that sits against the wall. Everything about building one follows from those three parts. This lesson gives you the vocabulary, then sends you downstairs with a tape measure.

Mission link. You already own two unfinished triangles and a stack of gym panels. Before we can decide what to do with them (finish, mount, cut up), you need to be able to look at a volume and say what it is made of and how it goes together.

The parts

Face
One flat plywood panel you can climb on. A triangle volume has three faces; a pyramid has four. Faces are cut from ¾" (nominal, about 18 mm) plywood in both the classic home build1 and commercial spec sheets2. Thinner ply flexes and lets T-nuts pull through.
Edge
Where two faces meet. On a good volume the edge is a mitered joint: each face is cut with a bevel (an angled edge, not a square one) so the two bevels close up into a tight seam. The bevel angle depends on the angle between the faces; that geometry is its own lesson. A cheaper volume uses a butt joint, where one square-cut face simply overlaps the other, leaving an exposed ply end. Both work; miters look and feel better.
Base (footprint)
The open back. Its outline is the shape the volume leaves on the wall. The base edges have to sit flat on the wall, so they are cut at an angle too, or trimmed after assembly1. If the base rocks, the volume flexes and the screws work loose.
Mounting screws
Plywood volumes are almost always screw-on: screws go through the faces, near the base, into the wall's plywood. The classic home build uses at least 8 screws, at least 3 per side1; Xcult specifies 4.5 to 5 mm screws with smooth (unribbed) heads2. Bolt-on mounting through T-nuts in the wall is common on fiberglass volumes but rare on wood.
T-nuts
Threaded inserts hammered or screwed into the back of a face so that bolt-on holds can attach. ⅜"-16 in the US, M10 in Europe2. Andy Librande's triangle used about 30, kept 2" back from the base edge so they stay clear of the mounting-screw zone and the joint1. Holds can also be screwed straight into the face (screw-on holds), so T-nuts are optional.
Texture
The gritty surface. It is sand in paint: roughly 10 parts paint to 1 part sand by volume for a home wall3; quartz sand under polyurethane paint for commercial volumes2. Raw plywood is slick and splinters, so an unfinished volume is not climbable yet.
Nominal vs actual: "¾ inch" plywood usually measures 23/32" (about 18.2 mm). Put a tape on the edge of your triangles: if it reads closer to ½" (12 mm) they are lighter-duty than a gym volume.

Your task: inspect what you own

In the basement, with a tape measure and a flashlight

Do this for each Marketplace triangle, then one gym panel. Write the answers down (a photo of a notebook page is fine); bring them back to your teacher.

  1. Faces. How many? Sketch the base outline and note which faces are the same size.
  2. Thickness. Measure the ply at an exposed edge. ¾" (18 mm)? ½" (12 mm)? Something else?
  3. Edges. Look at a seam. Is it a miter (two bevels meeting in a clean line, no end-grain showing) or a butt joint (one face's end-grain visible along the seam)? Any gaps?
  4. Fasteners. How are the faces held together? Count screws per seam. Any glue visible inside?
  5. Base. Set it on a flat floor. Does it rock? Do the base edges sit flat, or are they square-cut and touching the floor on one corner only?
  6. T-nuts. Any installed? What size (drop a ⅜" hold bolt in if you have one)? How far from the edges?
  7. Gym panel. Thickness, texture (sandy paint? something harder?), T-nut spacing and condition, and any framing or cleats still attached to the back. Photograph front and back.

Ten minutes per object is plenty. You are not fixing anything yet; you are learning to read a volume.
